Your First 30 Days After Separation: A Money Checklist

By Melissa White, Mortgage Broker & Finance Specialist, MoneyQuest Broadbeach · 20 October 2026

The first few weeks after separation can feel like a blur. There's so much to sort out, emotionally and practically, and money is often the part that keeps you up at night.

You don't need to fix everything at once. What helps most is knowing what to do first. Here's the simple, week-by-week checklist I share with women who come to me in those early days, so you can protect yourself, see where you stand, and make decisions from a steadier place.

Week 1: Protect yourself

The first job is making sure you can pay for the basics and that your money is safe. Start here:

  • Open a bank account in your own name. Then have your pay, and any government payments, paid into it. Ideally choose a bank your ex doesn't use.
  • Change your passwords and PINs. Include online banking, email, myGov and any shared apps or cloud storage, and update your account recovery details.
  • Check who can access what. Look at joint accounts and redraw facilities on your home loan. Ask your bank what options it has, such as needing both of you to approve withdrawals.
  • Keep copies of key documents somewhere safe. That includes ID, recent payslips, tax returns, bank and loan statements, and any insurance or super details.

Week 2: Get the full picture

It's hard to plan when you're not sure what you have or what you owe. This week, write it all down in one place:

  • Every account and debt. Your home loan, car loan, personal loans, credit cards, and buy now pay later accounts. Note which are in your name, your ex's name, or both.
  • Joint debts. On a joint loan, each of you is usually responsible for the whole amount, not half. If a joint credit card is still open, talk to the lender about closing it or stopping new spending.
  • Your credit report. You can get your credit report free once every three months from each credit reporting body, such as Equifax and Experian. Check it line by line for anything you don't recognise.
  • Your super. Note your balance, and check who you've nominated to receive it if you die.

Week 3: Build a bare-bones budget

Now work out what life costs on one income. Don't aim for perfect, aim for honest.

  • List your income. Your pay, plus any child support or government payments.
  • List your essentials first. Housing, food, utilities, transport, insurance, minimum debt repayments, and the costs of children in your care.
  • Cut or pause the non-essentials for now, such as subscriptions you don't use. You can add things back later.
  • Tell Services Australia. If you receive any Centrelink payments or family assistance, Services Australia asks you to let them know within 14 days of separating, so you're paid the right amount. Ask what you may now be eligible for, and about child support arrangements if you have children.

If the numbers don't add up and bills are piling up, the National Debt Helpline (1800 007 007) offers free, confidential financial counselling.

Week 4: Line up the right help

By now you'll have a much clearer picture. This is the time to get the right people in your corner, before you agree to anything.

  • A family lawyer. They can explain your rights, how property and super may be divided, and the time limits that apply. For married couples, an application for property orders generally has to be made within 12 months of a divorce taking effect. For de facto couples, it's generally within two years of separating.
  • A financial counsellor or adviser, if you need help with debts or longer-term planning.
  • A mortgage broker, if your home or a future home is part of the picture. Knowing what you could borrow on your own helps you and your lawyer weigh up your options.
  • Keep paying the home loan and other joint debts while things are being worked out. Missed repayments can show up on both of your credit files. If repayments are getting hard, speak to your lender early about hardship options.

A tip worth knowing

Try not to sign anything, take on new debt, or apply for lots of new credit in these first weeks. Each credit application can be recorded on your credit file, and it's much easier to make good decisions once you know exactly where you stand.
