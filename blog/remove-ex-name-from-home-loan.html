Why Your Bank Won't Just Take Your Ex's Name Off the Loan

By Melissa White, Mortgage Broker & Finance Specialist, MoneyQuest Broadbeach · 9 October 2026

You've agreed you'll keep the home. Your ex has moved out. So surely it's just a bit of paperwork: ring the bank, take their name off, done?

It's one of the first questions I hear from women after separation, and it makes complete sense. Unfortunately, it rarely works that way. Here's why, what actually happens, and how to give yourself the best chance of keeping your home on your own terms.

Why it isn't a simple name change

When you took out your home loan together, you didn't each agree to pay half. You both agreed to be responsible for the whole debt. The lender approved it based on two incomes and two people standing behind it.

Taking one name off means the lender gives up one of the people it relies on for repayment. So it treats the change as a new lending decision, not an admin update. That means:

  • The lender has to agree. Neither you nor your ex can remove a borrower on your own.
  • Your settlement doesn't decide it for the bank. Your property settlement sets out who keeps what between the two of you. The loan contract is between both of you and the lender, so the lender still has to approve the change.
  • You'll be assessed on your own. The real question becomes: can you comfortably manage this loan on your income alone?

What actually happens

There are usually two routes:

  1. 1
    Your current lender assesses you to take over the loan alone. Some lenders can release one borrower this way; others will ask for a full new application.
  2. 2
    You refinance into your sole name, with your current lender or a new one. This is common when you're also buying out your ex's share, because you'll usually need to borrow more.

Either way, the lender will look at:

  • Your income on your own and, depending on the lender, other income such as child support or Centrelink payments
  • Your living expenses, including the costs of children in your care
  • Any other debts in your name, including joint credit cards or car loans
  • Your credit history
  • A current valuation of the home and your equity (borrowing more than 80% of its value may mean paying lenders mortgage insurance)
  • Your separation documents, such as consent orders or a binding financial agreement
  • Your ex-partner's agreement to be released from the loan

Until it's done, you're both still responsible

This is the part that catches many women out. Until the lender formally releases one of you, you're both still fully liable for the loan. That's true even if one of you has moved out, and even if your settlement says otherwise.

Missed repayments can show up on both of your credit files. So keep repayments up while things are being sorted. If that's getting hard, talk to your lender early: lenders have hardship options, and asking sooner gives you more choices.

What about the title and stamp duty?

The loan and the title (who owns the property) are separate, and both usually need updating. A conveyancer or solicitor handles the title.

In Queensland, a property transfer made under a family law court order or financial agreement may be exempt from transfer duty (stamp duty). Whether it applies depends on having the right documents in place, so confirm with your conveyancer or solicitor before anything is transferred.

What if you can't qualify on your own?

It's not necessarily the end of the road. Depending on your situation, options can include:

  • A different lender. Lenders vary in which income they accept and how they view a recent separation.
  • A guarantor, such as a family member, where a lender allows it.
  • Restructuring the loan, for example a longer term to bring repayments down.
  • Revisiting the numbers in your settlement with your family lawyer.
  • Selling and starting fresh. Sometimes that's the option that gives you the most freedom, and that's okay too.

A tip worth knowing

Settlement orders often set a deadline for refinancing and paying out your ex. Find out what you can borrow on your own before your settlement is finalised, so you're not agreeing to something the numbers can't support.
